[ Project ]  CoreChoice

CoreChoice

Take the Big Five personality test once — free, and free to keep. Then bring it the decision you have been turning over for a week, and get advice written for how you decide, rather than advice in general.

Tester opt-in → In closed testing on Google Play — Android, invitation only for now. The test and your full result are free, and stay free.
[ 01 ]  Why

The problem is not a lack of options

People who agonise over decisions are rarely short of information. They have read the reviews, made the spreadsheet, asked three friends and received three answers. What they are short of is a way to stop — and every extra option makes stopping harder, not easier.

So CoreChoice does not hand you a longer list. It asks who you are first, then gives one recommendation with the reasoning laid out, and tells you plainly when a decision is genuinely too close to call. The answer screen closes with a line that is doing more work than it looks: you can sit with this, it will be here in the morning. That is the permission a maximiser never gives themselves.

[ 02 ]  What’s interesting

The result is free, and that is structural

The usual shape of a personality app is ten minutes of questions followed by a paywall standing between you and your own answers. CoreChoice does not do that, and it is not a promise in the marketing copy — the screen that shows your profile has no dependency on the coin ledger at all, so a paywall could not be added there without rewriting it. The result renders on a phone with no signal, and finishing the test adds five free analyses on top.

Nothing you type is stored on the server. The dilemma and your trait scores travel in the request body, are held in memory long enough to build the prompt, and are gone — what persists is a coin balance against a random identifier, a salted IP hash for abuse limits, and a usage row for cost tracking. There is no account, no email and no sign-in anywhere in the app.

The third rule is the one nobody notices when it is working: a coin is never lost to a failure of ours. Every path that spends one puts it back if anything downstream falls over — the model timing out, a response that cannot be parsed, even the phone hanging up mid-request. Two bugs of exactly that shape surfaced during the build, which is why it is now held in place by tests rather than good intentions.

The measurement is the real thing: the fifty-item IPIP Big-Five Factor Markers, which are public domain, with the reverse-keyed items kept honest by tests that fail if the keying drifts. The scores never reach you as percentages either. A number implies something is missing out of a hundred, and to someone already prone to over-deciding, “75%” reads as a one-in-four chance of ruining their life — so four plain labels replace it, and a trait is drawn as a point on a line rather than a bar that looks part-empty.
